# Kmart — Keep Your Secret

Draft copy for review. Nothing here is on the site yet.

Voice notes I worked to: first person, no em dashes, no balanced aphorisms, no
invented facts. Everything below comes from Anna's account of the weekend plus
the original concept description and the recovered source files. Where she said
she doesn't remember, the copy says so rather than filling the gap.

---

**Eyebrow:** DraftFCB Chicago · 2010 · New business pitch

**Headline:** Kmart had always sold clothes. Nobody had a reason to look again.

**Deck:** A new in-house design team, a studio in New York, a genuine relaunch of
the clothing line. The product had changed. The assumption about it had not.

---

## The brief

Target had spent years and a lot of money on its design department, and it had
worked. People went to Target on purpose, for the design, and said so afterwards.

Kmart was trying to do the same thing, and this part was real. They had hired a
team of fashion designers, put them in a studio in New York, and were relaunching
the clothing as something worth choosing rather than something that happened to
be on the shelf.

None of it was public yet. The designers were hired and the studio was working,
but the lines had not launched. The work up for grabs was the campaign that would
introduce them, and several agencies were pitching for it.

Which is worth sitting with for a second, because it means we were building a
campaign about a secret for a product that still was one.

The problem was that nothing about it read as news.

Kmart had always sold clothing, so "Kmart sells clothes" was not an announcement.
What was new was that the clothes were now designed, and nobody had any reason to
go and check.

Nobody arrived intending to buy clothes there either. The people who bought them
were in the store for something else, passed a rack, and picked something up
because it was in front of them and they needed it. A discovery, not a
destination.

And the people who did buy them were embarrassed to say where they came from.

So the product was not the problem. The problem was reappraisal. Kmart needed a
reason for somebody to look again on purpose, and it needed that reason to
survive being said out loud.

This pitch sat at the very front of that reinvention, before any of it was
public.

## The room

DraftFCB responded by flying in writers, designers, art directors and creative
directors from offices across its global network. Italy, France, Argentina, Germany. About fifteen people in
the Chicago office over a long weekend, starting Thursday and pitching Monday
night.

We were split into three groups of three or four. Each group took the same brief
from a different angle. It was not a competition between us. Creative directors
moved between the teams, and their job was less to judge than to keep the three
stories from converging. On the last day we workshopped all three together and
sharpened them into what got presented.

I was brought in for my fashion background. I had spent years designing apparel
graphics, hangtags and labels in Italy and Spain, and I knew that market,
including how it worked in Europe. That was the reason I was in the room.

The format was closer to how I like to work than anything I have done since.
Brainstorm as a group, break apart, build, come back, critique, break apart
again. Someone ordered Chinese food. It ran for four days and I have rarely
enjoyed work more.

## The idea

We stopped trying to argue anyone out of the embarrassment and made it the
mechanic instead.

**Keep Your Secret.** Fashion advertising, in the aspirational titles, showing
women who look like they have something they are not telling you. The line under
each one is a confession that has nothing to do with clothes.

> \* Wrote a screenplay about cat astronauts
> \* Some things are best kept to yourself.
> **Keep Your Secret.**

The secret is not that the dress came from Kmart. That is the joke, and it means
the campaign never has to say the embarrassing part out loud.

Underneath it sat **SHHH.com**, a members-only site behind a password. Exclusive
online deals, must-have pieces, trend and news content, and a network of other
people in on it.

That is the part that answers the actual brief. The ads buy you awareness, but a
password is a reason to go somewhere on purpose. You cannot stumble into a
members-only site the way you stumble into a rack on the way to the checkout.
Membership also flips the stigma rather than denying it. The point is no longer
that nobody can know. It is that not everybody does.

## Across every surface

The device that held it together was duct tape. Tape over the thing you do not
want read.

I do not remember who said it first. That weekend was continuous riffing and
nobody owned a single idea. What I can tell you is where mine came from. I had
spent years making hangtags, woven labels and in-store graphics for clothing
brands, so I was thinking about the object in the customer's hand, and the tape
went onto the parts of that object that name the retailer.

- **The bag.** Pink, and the Kmart logo covered with tape reading SHHH.COM.
- **The K.** The Kmart mark rebuilt out of duct tape.
- **Hangtags.** Tape over the label, peeling off to reveal the Kmart logo underneath.
- **Print.** The Keep Your Secret ads, running in Vogue and similar.
- **The site.** Login, then a members' homepage with look of the day, exclusives, video and the community.
- **Popup stores.** See below. This is the payoff, not a line item.

## The reveal

Popup stores in major cities, stocked entirely with the new Kmart lines. Nothing
in the shop branded Kmart. Every hangtag covered with a strip of duct tape.

You shop the rails and judge the clothes on the clothes. You buy something. It
goes into a bag with the tape over the label. You leave. And somewhere outside,
you peel the tape off and find out you just bought it from Kmart.

This is the part the whole campaign is built to arrive at, and it is why the tape
is the device rather than a graphic style. Every other surface is rehearsing the
same move at lower stakes. The ads withhold the secret. The site puts it behind a
password. The bag holds it until you are out of the shop.

1. **Nothing is branded.** An unbranded shop, no logo in the room.
2. **Every label is taped.** You have nothing to judge but the garment.
3. **You buy on the clothes alone.** The decision gets made before the brand enters it.
4. **The reveal happens outside.** Too late to be embarrassed about a choice you already made on merit.

It answers the brief exactly. Nobody had a reason to look again, so the store
removes the thing that stops people looking, gets an honest verdict on the
product, and only then puts the name back on it.

I built the site comps, the bag, and the print layouts. The writers worked the
copy. We put it in front of each other several times a day.

The membership mechanic is the part I would defend hardest now. Gated deals,
insider content, a social layer, all of that was still new for a mass retailer in
2010. The idea was not a website with a campaign attached. It was a brand you
had to be let into.

## The other route

The same group also explored **MARKT**, a harder rebrand that moved further from
the Kmart name, with SHHH living inside it as a section alongside Look of the
Day and a member gallery. It is the more cautious idea of the two and it did not
go as far.

## What happened

Three routes went to the client. A safe one, a middle one, and ours. We were the
far end of the scale, and I think part of the reason ours was presented at all
was to show how far the territory could go.

I did not present. I made the work.

I do not know which direction the client chose, or whether any of this ended up
anywhere. Sixteen years later I am not going to invent an outcome I never saw.
What I know is what the pitch was, what I made, and what it felt like to make it.

What I can point at is where the brand went. Kmart kept pushing on exactly this
problem, and by 2013 it was launching named celebrity clothing lines, including
one with Nicki Minaj, so that people would come for the clothing by name rather
than find it on the way to something else. I am not going to claim our weekend
caused that, because I have no idea whether it did. But the argument we were
making in 2010 was the same argument: the product had already changed, and what
was missing was a reason to arrive on purpose. We were three years early to a
position the brand eventually took.

## What I took from it

Two things.

The first is about the room. Fifteen people flown in from four countries, all of
them good, all of them glad to be there, all pointed at one problem for four
days. I had not seen before that it was possible to assemble that and have it
work. I have wanted to be back in that room ever since.

The second is more personal. I was picked for the detour. The years in Italy and
Spain designing clothes, the sewing, the fashion masters I paid for by taking
advertising contracts between terms, all of it read on paper as time spent away
from the career. On that project it was the reason I was useful.

Nobody could have told me that in advance, and I would not have believed it. But
it is the thing I have relied on ever since: that trends and objects and
interfaces all come from the same human place, and that knowing more than one
field is what lets you see it.
